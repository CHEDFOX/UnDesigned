# Bauhaus: research dossier

## 1. Definition

**Bauhaus** here means the graphic language of the German art school (Weimar 1919–1925, Dessau 1925–1932, Berlin 1932–1933) and the "New Typography" it helped shape. It builds pages instead of decorating them:
- elementary forms: circle, square, triangle and bar
- black and white with one or two strong primaries, flat
- asymmetric layouts on a grid, with heavy bars, thin rules and a strong diagonal
- bold sans-serif type, sometimes all lowercase, sometimes running up an edge
- photographs used as part of the typography (typophoto)

Its aim is **clarity through construction**: a piece that is read in a glance because every part has a job, and whose precision shows that someone thought it through.

It sits between two failure modes:

| Too cold | Bauhaus | Pastiche |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate grid with no idea, grey and timid; structure with nothing to say | One message, one focal form, strong colour, bold type on a clear grid | Red square, yellow triangle, blue circle as wallpaper; "Bauhaus" novelty fonts; copies of famous posters |

## 2. Lineage

### 2.1 The school and its aims

| Who / what | When | What we take |
|---|---|---|
| **Walter Gropius**, founding manifesto and programme of the Staatliches Bauhaus, Weimar | April 1919 | The building as the goal of all creative work; no essential difference between artist and craftsman. Design as a collaborative, useful craft rather than ornament. Source of **construction, not decoration**. |
| **Three cities, three directors**: Gropius (1919–28), Hannes Meyer (1928–30), Ludwig Mies van der Rohe (1930–33) | 1919–1933 | Weimar (1919–25), Dessau (1925–32; Gropius's building opened 1926), Berlin (1932–33). The Dessau council voted to close the school in August 1932 at the Nazi Party's request; after a police search in April 1933 the masters dissolved it in July 1933. |
| **"Art and technology: a new unity"** | from 1923 | The Dessau-era turn to industrial production. Source of **machine precision** in our forms and motion. |
| **Oskar Schlemmer**, Bauhaus signet | adopted 1922, used as emblem from 1923 | A face built from a few geometric parts. We learn from the method and never copy the mark. |

### 2.2 Graphic design: the key figures and works

| Who / what | When | What we take |
|---|---|---|
| **Joost Schmidt**, poster for the first Bauhaus exhibition, Weimar | July–September 1923 | Lettering and geometric forms combined into one composition, reflecting the school's new focus on typography and Schlemmer's influence. Schmidt later led the printing and advertising workshop in Dessau. Source of **forms carrying type**. (Our single-diagonal rule is a rule of thumb from the period's posters and covers, not from this one work.) |
| **László Moholy-Nagy**, essay "Die neue Typographie" in the catalogue *Staatliches Bauhaus in Weimar 1919–1923* | 1923 | Typography as communication "in its most intense form", with the emphasis on absolute clarity; letters must not be forced into an a-priori frame. Source of **clarity first**. |
| **Herbert Bayer** (cover) and **Moholy-Nagy** (layout), the 1923 catalogue | 1923 | Square format, red and blue blocks of letters on a black cover, unusual text–image relationships. Source of **few strong colours** and **type as structure**. |
| **Herbert Bayer**, emergency banknotes for the State Bank of Thuringia | August 1923 | No coats of arms or ornament: the value as the dominant element, sans-serif type, saturated colour fields that changed in a short cycle. Source of **the large numeral as image** and the colour `cycle` move. |
| **Bauhausbücher**, 14 volumes edited by Gropius and Moholy-Nagy (Albert Langen, Munich) | 1925–1930 | Book design led by Moholy-Nagy; covers built from bars, photographs and sans type. Source of **bars and rules** and **typophoto**. |
| **Moholy-Nagy**, *Malerei, Fotografie, Film* (Bauhausbücher 8), where he set out "typophoto" | 1925 | Type and photograph as one visual statement. Source of our **photomontage** rules. |
| **Herbert Bayer**, universal alphabet; the Bauhaus adopts lowercase-only writing (*kleinschreibung*) | 1925 | A single-case geometric alphabet from arcs, angles and straight lines, never cast as metal type. Bayer argued one case saves time and money. Source of **lowercase headlines as an option** and **geometric letterforms**. |
| **Printing and advertising workshop**, Dessau (Bayer, then Schmidt) | 1925–1932 | Standardised, uniform typography; later Bauhaus print often used existing jobbing grotesques rather than special fonts. Source of our preference for **sturdy grotesques and geometric sans** over novelty faces. |
| **Ludwig Hirschfeld-Mack**, coloured light plays; **Moholy-Nagy**, *Light Prop for an Electric Stage* | 1922–23; 1930 | Moving circles, triangles and squares in red, yellow and blue; motorised, mechanical movement. Source of **mechanical motion**. |

### 2.3 Colour and form teaching

| Who / what | When | What we take |
|---|---|---|
| **Wassily Kandinsky**, questionnaire for the wall-painting workshop | 1923 | Students were asked to pair yellow, red and blue with triangle, square and circle and give reasons. The published result (yellow triangle, red square, blue circle) shaped the school's murals. Later research found the pairing is not universal: in a replication with 200 students, Kandinsky's set was the *least* preferred; red triangle, blue square, yellow circle was most common (Jacobsen, 2002). We use **colour by role** (accent on the focal form), not by Kandinsky's code. |
| **Josef Albers**, taught the preliminary course at the Bauhaus; later *Interaction of Color* (Yale, 1963) | 1920s–1963 | Colour is relative: it changes with its neighbours. Source of our rule to judge colours **in the actual combination**, and to keep area ratios unequal. |

### 2.4 What came after (legacy, not Bauhaus itself)

- **Jan Tschichold**, *Die neue Typographie* (1928): codified asymmetric, sans-serif typography for print.
- **Futura** (Paul Renner, Bauer, 1927): the best-known geometric sans of the period. Renner was not a Bauhaus teacher; Futura shares the geometric ideal. Jost (our new pairing) is an original interpretation of 1920s German sans-serifs in that tradition.
- **ITC Bauhaus** (Ed Benguiat and Victor Caruso, 1975): a commercial display face modelled on Bayer's universal alphabet. Its rounded, novelty character reads as theme-party Bauhaus; we avoid it.
- "Form follows function" is **Louis Sullivan's** phrase (1896), not a Bauhaus slogan.

## 3. Why it works: evidence

The findings are in `foundations/research/visual-preference.json`. Bauhaus is honest about where it goes against them.

| Effect (finding id) | Finding | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Low complexity** (`simplicity`) | Pages with low visual complexity are rated more appealing, and complexity is judged within 17–50 ms (Tuch et al., 2012). | Five forms at most, flat colour and a grid keep complexity low. This is where Bauhaus is strongest. |
| **Fluency** (`fluency`) | Clarity, contrast, figure-ground separation and repetition make things easier to process and so more liked (Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, 2004). | Strong contrast, a grid and a fixed set of repeated forms are the style. |
| **First impression** (`first-impression`) | Visual appeal is judged consistently after 50 ms (Lindgaard et al., 2006). | One large focal form plus a big headline work before anyone reads. |
| **Curvature** (`curvature`): **goes against** | People prefer curved contours to sharp angles, a medium, reliable effect (g = 0.39 across 61 studies), possibly because sharp angles read as threat (Bar & Neta, 2006; Chuquichambi et al., 2022). Downward-pointing V shapes activate brain regions linked to threat detection (Larson et al., 2009). | Squares, triangles, sharp corners and diagonals lower average liking. Guards: the circle is the default focal form; at most one triangle; triangles point up or along the diagonal, never down at the reader; corners stay square but forms stay few. We accept a lower liking score to gain attention and distinctiveness (below). |
| **Handmade** (`handmade`): **goes against** | Handmade objects are seen as more attractive because they seem to carry love (Fuchs, Schreier & van Osselaer, 2015). | Bauhaus is deliberately machine-made. The care has to show in precision and in the idea, and warmth has to come from the copy and photography of real people. |
| **Natural detail** (`fractals`): **goes against** | Mid-range fractal roughness (D 1.3–1.5) is preferred (Spehar et al., 2003). | Exact geometry has none. Photographs (typophoto) are where natural detail can enter. |
| **Colour valence** (`colour-valence`) | Colour preference follows object associations: blues are among the most liked, dark yellows and olives among the least (Palmer & Schloss, 2010). | Strong blue and clean yellow are safe; avoid the browner Wada reds and ochres as large grounds. |
| **Familiar + fresh** (`maya`) | Preference peaks where typicality and novelty balance (Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen, 2003). | Bauhaus is highly typical, so it risks pastiche. Put the novelty in the idea, the photograph and the product's own forms, never in more Bauhaus clichés. |

### What Bauhaus wins that liking scores miss

| Win | Evidence | How we use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Distinctiveness and memory** | An item that differs from its surroundings is remembered better (isolation or von Restorff effect; von Restorff, 1933; reviewed by Hunt, 1995). | In a market of rounded, pastel brand styles, hard geometry and primaries stand out. Keep the rest of the piece quiet so the one focal form isolates. |
| **Attention and direction** | Angular and pointed shapes are processed as more arousing and threat-relevant (Bar & Neta, 2006; Larson et al., 2009). | One diagonal and one triangle direct the eye toward the headline or call to action. Used once, they guide; used everywhere, they repel. |
| **Structure and trust** | Grids, contrast and repetition increase fluency (Reber et al., 2004); attractive interfaces are perceived as easier to use (Kurosu & Kashimura, 1995). | The visible order reads as competence: useful for architecture, engineering, education, furniture and culture. |
| **Cultural meaning** | The Bauhaus is one of the most widely recognised design schools (Bauhaus Kooperation; Britannica). | The look signals "modern, rational, well made". That meaning is an asset only for products that can live up to it. |

Our scores on the seven criteria (0–2): curves 1, simplicity 2, familiar + fresh 1, natural detail 0, handmade 0, liked colours 1, 50 ms clarity 2. The notes are in `approach.json` → `evidence`. These are our synthesis, not a published ranking.

## 4. What it is and isn't

| It is | It isn't |
|---|---|
| One message, one focal form in the accent colour | Kandinsky's three coloured shapes as decoration |
| Circle, square, triangle, bars; exact, flat, no outline | Freeform blobs, outlines, rounded corners, gradients |
| Angles 0°, 90° and one 45° diagonal | Many angles, tilted everything |
| Asymmetric, on an 8-column grid; forms bleed off an edge | Centred, symmetrical, framed |
| Black, white and one Wada combination with a strong primary | Equal amounts of red, yellow and blue; pastels |
| Bold geometric or grotesque sans; sentence case or all lowercase | ITC Bauhaus, stencils, serifs, scripts, all-caps headlines |
| Black-and-white photos cropped hard and built into the type | Colour stock photos with soft masks |
| Crisp, critically damped motion along an axis; 90° turns | Bounces, wobbles, organic morphs |
| Direct, factual copy | "Form follows function" slogans, German words as decoration |

## 5. Risks and how we guard against them

| Risk | Guard |
|---|---|
| Pastiche: looks like a Bauhaus souvenir | Never copy historical posters, the Schlemmer signet or the Kandinsky triad; use colour by role; forms come from the product's own message (`art.json` → `method`, `vocabulary.avoid`) |
| Cold or hostile (curvature, handmade findings) | Circle as the default focal form; at most one triangle, never pointing down at the reader; warmth from the copy and from photographs of real people and products |
| Every piece looks the same | Change the focal form and the placement (`bleed`, `diagonal`, `split`, `typophoto`) per piece; the idea leads |
| Low contrast in saturated pairs (red on blue, black on red) | Type uses the combination's text role, or black or white, whichever passes AA; prefer combinations marked `body` (`art.json` → `palette`) |
| Too busy: many forms, many bars | Five forms and four bars at most; at least 30% empty ground; squint test |
| Garish colour | Accent on one form; unequal areas (ground 45–65%, accent 10–25%); full triad only with combination 154 |
| Motion feels robotic in a bad way | Critically damped springs (they still ease out), one move at a time, short loops that end on the finished composition |

## 6. Scorecard

Score each piece 0 (no), 1 (partly) or 2 (yes). 16 or more out of 20 is ready.

1. Says one thing; readable in 2 seconds at thumbnail size.
2. One focal form carries the idea and the accent colour.
3. Built only from circle, square, triangle, bars (and half or quarter circles); five forms or fewer.
4. Angles are 0°, 90° and at most one 45° diagonal.
5. Asymmetric and on the grid; at least one form or bar meets the edge of the sheet.
6. One Wada combination, black and white; area ratios within `art.json` → `palette.roles`; no gradients or shadows.
7. Bars use the three fixed weights; no outlines, no rounded corners.
8. Type is the product's pairing, bold sans headline, left-aligned, three sizes at most, AA contrast.
9. Motion (if any) is crisp, axis-bound, without overshoot, ends on a still frame and has a reduced-motion fallback.
10. Not pastiche: no copied historical marks, no Kandinsky triad as decoration, no novelty "Bauhaus" font.
