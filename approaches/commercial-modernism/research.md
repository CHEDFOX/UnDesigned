# Commercial Modernism: research dossier

## 1. Definition

**Commercial Modernism** is modern art put to work selling things. Between the first object posters in Berlin (about 1905) and the end of the 1940s, poster artists and art directors took ideas from Cubism, Futurism, Constructivism and the Bauhaus and used them to advertise railways, liners, aperitifs, matches, electricity and paperboard. Its aim is to make **one product look powerful and modern in a single glance**:
- one object, shown big, from a bold viewpoint
- depth from perspective and smooth airbrushed modelling, inside hard stencil edges
- geometric lettering built into the picture, with the brand name set large

It sits between two failure modes:

| Too dry | Commercial Modernism | Too costume |
|---|---|---|
| Product shot on white, logo in the corner, no viewpoint and no energy | One hero object, a dramatic viewpoint, airbrushed volume, a big brand name, a short slogan | Fake-aged "vintage" posters: paper stains, sunbursts as decoration, period slang, Art Deco ornament without an idea |

## 2. Lineage

### 2.1 The object poster (Sachplakat), Germany, 1905–1920s

| Who / what | When | What we take |
|---|---|---|
| **Lucian Bernhard**, poster for Priester matches | c. 1905 (some sources say 1906) | Entered for a competition; Bernhard is said to have removed element after element from his first sketch (accounts mention dancing girls, an ashtray and a cigar) until only two matches and the brand name were left. The judge Ernst Growald is reported to have retrieved it from the rejects. Source of **CM1, the product is the hero**, and of the reduction recipe in `art.json` → `method`. Bernhard moved to New York in 1923. |
| **Ludwig Hohlwein**, Munich | from c. 1906 | Object posters with more depth: pattern, texture and light on flat grounds, influenced by the Beggarstaffs' cut-paper designs. Cooper Hewitt notes his influence on Cassandre and Paul Rand. **See section 5:** Hohlwein joined the Nazi party and made propaganda posters; we take his early commercial method only. |

### 2.2 The modernist poster, France and Britain, 1915–1940

| Who / what | When | What we take |
|---|---|---|
| **E. McKnight Kauffer**, posters for the London Underground, commissioned by Frank Pick | 1915–1940 | More than 100 Underground posters over 25 years (London Transport Museum; Cooper Hewitt counts about 150 including car-cards). Modern art (Vorticism, Cubism) used for a public client. |
| Kauffer, *Soaring to Success! Daily Herald – the Early Bird* | 1919 | A flock of geometric birds rising on a diagonal, from his 1916 woodcut *Flight* (V&A). Source of the **diagonal** placement and of repeated shapes as momentum. |
| **A. M. Cassandre**, *Étoile du Nord* (Chemin de Fer du Nord) | 1927 | Rails converging on a star at the vanishing point; lettering framing the image (V&A E.224-1935; MoMA). Source of the **tunnel** placement and of **lettering in perspective**. |
| Cassandre, *Dubonnet* ("Dubo, Dubon, Dubonnet") | 1932 | A serial poster: a figure gains colour and the word completes as he drinks. Described as among the first posters designed to be read from fast-moving vehicles. Source of the **serial** lettering zone and motion move. |
| Cassandre, *Normandie* (Compagnie Générale Transatlantique) | 1935 | The liner's bow seen head-on and from low down, looming over tiny gulls; the V&A describes it as probably his most iconic work and credits the frontal view with conveying the ship's scale (V&A E.648-2017; also Museo Reina Sofía). Source of the **monument** placement and of the low horizon. |
| Cassandre's methods | 1923–1936 | Gouache applied with brush, "paint gun" (airbrush) or stipple brush, with paper cut-outs as stencils (MoMA exhibition catalogue, 1936). Geometry from Cubism, softened by airbrushed blends. Source of **CM4, airbrush within hard edges**. |

### 2.3 The streamline decade, America, 1930–1945

| Who / what | When | What we take |
|---|---|---|
| **Streamline Moderne** (Raymond Loewy, Norman Bel Geddes, Henry Dreyfuss, Walter Dorwin Teague) | 1930s | Teardrops, long horizontal speed lines and tapering forms, borrowed from aerodynamics and applied to objects that never needed it (refrigerators, pencil sharpeners): styling that *looks* fast and modern. Source of the **streamline** shape family and of the style's motion character. |
| **Fortune** magazine (Henry Luce; first issue February 1930) | 1930s | Covers by commissioned artists, with T. M. Cleland setting the format; industry treated as a subject worthy of art. Source of the **corporate** use of the style. |
| **Herbert Matter**, posters for the Swiss National Tourist Office | 1934–1936 | Photomontage: a large close-up (a face, a hand) against a small Alpine scene, with typography. *All Roads Lead to Switzerland* (1935) takes Cassandre's steep perspective into photography. Source of the **montage** placement and its 4:1 scale jump. |
| **Lester Beall**, posters for the Rural Electrification Administration | 1937–1941 | Flat colour, arrows and stripes, photo cut-outs; one plain message per poster (*Light*, *Running Water*, *Radio*, 1937). MoMA holds several. Source of the restrained grey–red–black scheme and of the style's **public-service** voice. |
| **Alexey Brodovitch**, art director of *Harper's Bazaar* | 1934–1958 | Photographs, Bodoni type and white space composed across the double-page spread; Cassandre drew Bazaar covers 1937–40. Source of the **bodoni-jost** pairing and of editorial use. |
| **Container Corporation of America** (Walter Paepcke; art director Egbert Jacobson from 1936) | 1937–1950s | From 1937, ads by Cassandre, Jean Carlu, Herbert Bayer, Herbert Matter and others, with the agency N. W. Ayer's Charles Coiner: modern art to make a maker of paperboard boxes look sophisticated. The *Great Ideas of Western Man* series followed from 1950. Source of the idea that **a company can stand behind an image without showing a box**. |

### 2.4 What we keep and what we leave

We keep the method: one object, a viewpoint, airbrushed volume, lettering as part of the picture, few words. We leave the period's content: its colonial imagery, its glamour stereotypes and its heroic propaganda figures (section 5), and its physical wear (we design new pieces, not reproductions).

### 2.5 The boundary with Mid-century Modern

A separate approach covers Mid-century Modern (1950s–60s). The two overlap in time (CCA's *Great Ideas* ran 1950–75; Brodovitch worked until 1958), so we separate them by **method**, not by date:

| Commercial Modernism (this style) | Mid-century Modern (separate style) |
|---|---|
| Depth: perspective, low horizons, vanishing points | Flat: no perspective, shapes float on the ground |
| Airbrushed modelling inside hard edges | Flat colour; texture from print and hand |
| Machine-age polish, precise curves and diagonals | Playful, hand-cut, organic shapes (boomerangs, kidneys, starbursts) |
| The product as a monument | Wit and visual puns; the product often implied |
| Geometric sans and Art Deco display, tracked capitals | Lively type, hand lettering, mixed scripts |
| Travel, transport, energy, industry, premium goods | Consumer goods, culture, home, children |

If a piece is flat, witty and hand-cut, it belongs to Mid-century Modern. If it has a viewpoint, airbrushed volume and a monumental product, it belongs here.

## 3. Why it works: evidence

Finding ids refer to `foundations/research/visual-preference.json`.

| Effect | Finding | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| **One object reads fastest** (`simplicity`, `first-impression`) | Visual complexity is judged in 17–50 ms and low complexity is liked more (Tuch et al., 2012); appeal is judged consistently after 50 ms (Lindgaard et al., 2006). | The Sachplakat reduction (CM1) is the style's main asset: one silhouette and a name work before anyone reads. |
| **Clear figure and ground** (`fluency`) | Clarity, contrast and figure–ground separation increase liking (Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman, 2004). | Dark object on a light ground, or the reverse, with a solid lettering band. |
| **Familiar, with one twist** (`maya`) | Typicality and novelty together predict preference (Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen, 2003). The authors named the principle after Raymond Loewy's "Most Advanced, Yet Acceptable", a designer of this very period. | The format and the object are familiar; the surprise is the viewpoint. |
| **Pictures and words both earn attention** | In 1,363 print ads tested by eye-tracking, brand, picture and text each had their own effect; the picture captured attention independent of its size, and larger text areas and headlines raised total attention more than larger pictures (Pieters & Wedel, 2004). | A big object *and* big lettering (CM5). Don't let the picture squeeze the words. |
| **Clever design beats clutter** | Design complexity (an elaborate creative layout) raised attention and liking; feature complexity (dense detail, edges, colours) lowered brand attention (Pieters, Wedel & Batra, 2010). | Dramatic perspective is design complexity with few features. Keep details under the limit in `art.json` → `shape.detailCount`. |
| **Implied motion** | Still photos implying motion activated the motion-sensitive area MT/MST more than photos at rest (Kourtzi & Kanwisher, 2000); later work found low-level features contribute (Lorteije et al., 2011). | Diagonals, speed lines and rails (CM3) make a still poster feel like it moves. |

**Where the style goes against the evidence, and why that can be right:**

- **Curves** (`curvature`): people prefer curved to sharp-angled objects (Bar & Neta, 2006; meta-analysis g = 0.39, Chuquichambi et al., 2022), and a downward-pointing V is implicitly linked with unpleasant content (Larson, Aronoff & Steuer, 2012). This style uses sharp bows, wedges and rays. We guard it: two-thirds of the hero's outline is streamline curve, and sharp angles point *up* or *across*, not down at the viewer (section 5). The angles buy speed and drama, which liking scores do not measure.
- **Handmade** (`handmade`): the look aims at machine polish, so it gives up the warmth that visible hand marks add (Fuchs, Schreier & van Osselaer, 2015). That is the trade: it suits products whose promise is precision, speed or scale.
- **Nature** (`fractals`): smooth airbrush and precise curves have none of the mid-range roughness people like in natural scenes (Spehar et al., 2003). Keep the style for subjects where engineering is the point.

## 4. What it is and isn't

| It is | It isn't |
|---|---|
| One product or object as the hero, 40–65% of the height | A scene, a catalogue, or several products |
| A deliberate viewpoint: low horizon, vanishing point, or montage | A neutral eye-level product shot |
| Two-stop airbrush gradients between colours of one combination | Multi-colour gradients, glows, drop shadows, chrome |
| Hard, clean stencil edges | Outlines, wobble, brush texture, distressing |
| Geometric sans and one deco display word | Script, rounded, or "vintage" grunge fonts |
| Brand word in tracked capitals, slogan in sentence case | All-caps paragraphs, centred body text |
| Smooth, unhurried, overshoot-free motion | Bouncy springs, shakes, spins |
| New work built with a 1930s method | A reproduction or parody of a 1930s poster |

## 5. Risks and how we guard against them

| Risk | Guard |
|---|---|
| Reads as pastiche or nostalgia | No fake wear, no decorative sunbursts, no period slang; contemporary products and facts (CM7, `art.json` → `vocabulary.avoid`) |
| Propaganda overtones (heroic figures, black–white–red, marching masses) | No muscular "ideal" figures, no crowds as masses, no flag-waving. The V&A notes these as typical of Nazi imagery in Hohlwein's 1933 Winterhilfswerk poster. We cite Hohlwein only for his pre-1933 commercial work. |
| Colonial and racial stereotypes found in period ads | Never depict people or places as exotic decoration; show real places with respect, or use objects instead |
| Women as ornament | Show the product or the experience, not a glamour figure |
| Sharp angles feel aggressive | Two-thirds streamline curve on the hero; no downward-pointing wedges aimed at the viewer; diagonals rise (`art.json` → `shape.ratio`) |
| Gradients get muddy or off-palette | Exactly two stops from one combination, plus Black and White; at most 4 modelled objects (`art.json` → `airbrush`) |
| Low contrast between text and ground | Lettering on a solid band with White or the lightest colour; prefer combinations with light-mode `inkUse: "body"` |
| Deco display faces hurt legibility | Deco face for the brand word only, at 24 px / 18 pt and above (`typography.json`) |

## 6. Scorecard

Score each piece 0 (no), 1 (partly) or 2 (yes). 16 or more out of 20 is ready.

1. One hero object, recognisable as a solid silhouette at thumbnail size.
2. The object fills 40–65% of the canvas height (or is cropped by one edge).
3. One clear viewpoint: a deliberate horizon and consistent vanishing point(s).
4. At least one dominant diagonal or converging line set (15–40°).
5. Airbrush gradients: two stops, colours of one Wada combination only; none on lettering.
6. Hard, clean edges; no outlines, textures or fake wear.
7. Lettering sits in a planned zone and the brand name is large.
8. Slogan of 2–7 words, sentence case, with a specific fact; brand next to it.
9. Accent on one object, at the focal point; text contrast passes AA.
10. Motion (if any) glides without overshoot, one thing at a time, and ends on the full poster.
